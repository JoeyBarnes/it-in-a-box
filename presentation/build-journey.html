Building a reusable standard with Microsoft Scout

IT-in-a-Box

How a recurring assessment problem at an asset-management portfolio became a 141-capability standard, a published site, and a working method for building rich artifacts with an AI agent.

A repeating question→ Four false starts→ The reframe→ Audit & honesty→ Published standard

Scout is a desktop agent with access to the local filesystem, a shell, a browser and Microsoft 365 — so it reads, builds, tests and commits in place rather than handing back text to paste somewhere.

Framing: this is a story about building a reusable asset, not about a single document review. Keep the Scout one-liner to a single breath — the audience should learn what it is from watching what it did, not from a description. Press N for notes, O for the slide grid, T for theme.
The starting point

The problem was repetition, not analysis

The business context

An asset-management portfolio. Businesses join the portfolio — each arriving with a technology function already in place: its own platforms, its own vendors, its own decisions.

So the question is rarely "what is missing?"

The actual questions

  • How does this estate compare to the standard?
  • Where is convergence worth the cost?
  • Where is this business better left running its own?

Answering that from a blank page each time is slow — and produces answers that can't be compared across the portfolio.

The goal was never a deliverable. It was a reusable part — a standard that makes a recurring assessment repeatable.

Land this hard before anything else. Every design decision downstream follows from "reusable and comparable," not from "thorough." Thoroughness was the easy part.
Why this is harder than it sounds

Every obvious approach fails a portfolio

An org chart

Describes one company's structure. Two businesses with identical capability needs organise them differently — so nothing compares.

A product catalogue

Lists what Microsoft sells. Inverts the question: you end up assessing the estate against a price list rather than against what the business needs to operate.

A bespoke assessment

Accurate for one business, incomparable across five. And the effort is paid again every time.

What it had to be instead

A model of capabilities — what a business needs in order to operate — expressed as a standard, with each capability mapped to the Microsoft platform so that "what we already own" and "what still has to be bought" fall out of the same view.

That last clause is the commercial hinge: one artifact serves both the assessment conversation and the licensing conversation, because coverage and ownership are the same axis.

The turn — 19 August

Once the unit of the model became a capability rather than a team, a product or a project, everything else became decidable: what belongs, what it maps to, and how two businesses compare.

Name the moment plainly: the previous three attempts weren't bad executions of the right idea, they were good executions of the wrong unit. Changing the unit of the model is what unlocked it.
The hardest design decision

What earns a place in the model

A capability earns its place if it is a distinct estate category that an assessment must cover, and it is sized as one product-selection decision.

The weaker test I explicitly rejected

"A gap is only worth listing if a reader might assume Microsoft covers it."

Nobody assumes Microsoft sells SCADA — and 07.11 is in the model anyway. That test describes a marketing document, not an assessment standard.

Deliberate exclusions

TreasuryTaxTravel & expenseLearning managementInvestor reporting

Each is a module of something already modelled — ERP, HCM or BI. Breaking them out would turn area 07 into an application catalogue, which the compression exists to prevent.

This is the slide that shows the work was edited, not just generated. Writing down the test you rejected, and why, is what stops a model like this drifting into a product list six months later.
A second rule — and the one that transfers furthest

Every model meets a category that tries to eat it

A magnet category is usually a property of things you already have — not a class of new things. Give it a tag or a view, never a second home.

The pressure, in any model

Sooner or later a subject-flavoured category arrives: operational technology, AI, cloud, sustainability — whatever the budget is organised around this year.

It has real gravity: a dozen entries genuinely touch it. Give it a folder and the same capability gets two homes — exactly when comparison between businesses breaks.

Gravity is signal, not threat

That pull is evidence the category is real — real as a facet, a way people want to slice the model, rather than as a bucket.

So the answer isn't “no”. It's “yes, as a lens” — honouring the demand without fragmenting the model.

Every cross-cutting concern here is handled that way — all 141 capabilities still have exactly one home.

This is the rule I would carry to any taxonomy. Two caveats worth having ready if challenged. One: it only matters when the model exists for comparison — in a one-off narrative, duplication costs nothing. Two: sometimes the subject really is a new class, and the release valve is to ask whether it has its own budget, team, decision rights and vendors. If it does, "it's just security" may be the wrong answer. Also note the axis itself is derived from the model's purpose — function wins here because the job is cross-business comparison, not because function always wins.
21 August — a correction that changed the meaning of the data

A gap is not always a missing product

The problem

Some capabilities are a management discipline, not a purchase. Business continuity. Enterprise architecture. Risk management. Exit planning.

Marking those Gap implies Microsoft should sell something and doesn't — which is simply the wrong reading.

The fix

A Practice marker, applied to 8 capabilities:

"An operating discipline — coverage describes tooling support, not a deficiency."

Now Gap on a Practice capability reads correctly: you own this discipline; tooling assists it.

12.7 Business Continuity — "Genuine gap, and distinct from technical disaster recovery which Azure does cover. There is no business impact analysis, continuity planning or exercising capability."
Precision about what a rating means is worth more than more ratings. Five of the eight Practice capabilities are rated Gap — without the marker, each of those five reads as a Microsoft shortfall rather than as work the organisation owns.

Every capability carries the Microsoft product and its key components, a coverage rating, named non-Microsoft alternatives, a candid positioning statement, and a link to the authoritative Learn page.

12.6 Enterprise Architecture — "Genuine gap — Microsoft offers architecture guidance but no enterprise architecture repository, standards catalogue or design authority tooling." Alternatives: SAP LeanIX · Ardoq · Bizzdesign

A Microsoft-produced model that names LeanIX, Okta, Genesys and ServiceNow is one a client will actually believe — and that belief is what makes the Native ratings persuasive.

The honesty isn't altruism, it's engineering. 26 candid gaps are what make 60 Native ratings credible. A model that covered everything would be read as a brochure and discounted entirely.

Regulatory grounding

This is the entry to open when someone asks whether an AI-assisted model can be trusted. It argues against its own author's commercial interest, and it's right to.

How the content was produced

Researched against documentation, not recalled from memory

The working rule

Product names, components, licensing and coverage ratings were researched against current Microsoft documentation rather than generated from what the model remembered.

Every capability links to its Microsoft Learn reference, so any claim is one click from being checked.

21 & 26 August — two currency audits

"Add 3 capabilities and correct 5 mappings after currency audit"

"Correct stale product facts and three internal inconsistencies"

Products get renamed, repackaged and retired. Project for the web folding into Planner is exactly the kind of drift a re-audit catches.

And it says so in the footer. "Created with the assistance of Microsoft Scout, which uses AI… AI-assisted content can still contain errors or become outdated. Verify anything you intend to rely on for a decision."

Publishing the disclaimer was a deliberate choice, not a legal reflex. It sets the right contract with the reader: this is a well-researched starting point with checkable sources, not an oracle.
The artifact — Capability Model

141 capabilities — each carrying the same evidence

Point at a positioning statement and read it aloud. That sentence is the difference between a spreadsheet of product names and something an architect will defend in front of a client. Tiers matter for the portfolio use case: a small acquired business is assessed against Essential, not against all 141 — same model, proportionate conversation.
24 August — the problem I hit as a reader

Complete — and impossible to take in

What was wrong

The Capability Model was right: 141 entries, every one carrying a product, components, alternatives, positioning and a coverage rating.

It was also the landing page — so the first thing anyone saw was an accordion list 141 rows deep.

You could find anything in it. You could not see it. No shape, no proportion, no sense of where the weight sat.

What I asked for

Give me a way to visualise the whole thing on one page — the model is too dense to orient in.

Not less detail. A different altitude. Somewhere to stand before descending into 141 rows.

Four commits in forty minutes — and the third one is the tell

20:13  Add one-page stack overview graphic

"Always land on the Overview tab" is the fix stated out loud: orientation comes first, detail second. The density was never a content problem — it was a missing entry point.

Worth telling as a reader's experience, not a design critique: I built the thing and then couldn't use my own artifact. Completeness and comprehensibility are different properties, and an agent optimises the first unless you ask for the second. The commit log is unusually honest here — you can watch the fix land in four steps inside forty minutes.
24–25 August — the orientation view

The whole stack on one page

The shape carries an argument: security isn't a layer you can point at, and governance isn't a box — they qualify everything. The layout makes that claim before a single word is read.
A build decision worth stealing

The graphic is generated from the model — so it cannot drift

Model data→ Overview layout→ On-screen view→ Downloaded SVG / PNG

The usual failure

A diagram is drawn once in a design tool. The model moves. Six months later the picture in the deck contradicts the data in the tool, and nobody notices until a client does.

What we built instead

Every label, count and coverage bar is read from the model at render time. Download SVG and Download PNG generate from that same function, in whichever theme is active. There is no second copy to keep in step.

Even the committed snapshot files are produced by a script that drives the page's own generator in headless Edge.

This is the most portable idea in the talk. "Generate the picture from the data" costs an afternoon once and eliminates an entire category of embarrassment forever.

Two hours that stress-tested the standard

Why the loop matters

Every addition risks double-counting something already modelled. Left unresolved, those overlaps are what turn a standard into a list.

So the overlap gets fixed in the next commit, not the next month — and the rule that settled it gets written down while the argument is still fresh.

~30 capabilities added in an afternoon without losing coherence.

Two hours of sustained architectural argument. The value here was continuity — nothing dropped between commits, so an overlap introduced by a new area got caught in the same working session rather than surfacing weeks later. If asked what was hard: not generating the capabilities, but keeping 141 of them from quietly overlapping each other.
The proof

A standard is only credible if a real initiative fits it

The test

Take one real, MIP-protected architecture brief — Microsoft Fabric analytics and ERP synchronisation — and express it against the model.

Not summarise it. Not review it. Map it.

Local parsing failed — the document was sensitivity-labelled, so it was read through Word under my identity and the plaintext extract deleted.

What that proves in both directions

  • If a real initiative can be expressed in the model's vocabulary, the model is complete enough to be used
  • Coverage dots carry through, so a red dot marks where the design depends on something Microsoft does not provide
  • Where the initiative needed something the model lacked, the model got fixed
Clarify the role of the Word document if anyone asks: it wasn't the origin of the work, it was the test case that validated it. The standard came first.
The artifact — Reference Implementation

One initiative, expressed against the standard

Note the control planes start at the stage where they first apply rather than spanning everything decoratively — that placement is itself an architectural claim.
What mapping exposed

Findings the prose never made explicit

Two honest "you will build this"

05.8 — dispatch rows hold pointers into Bronze and the mapping registry assumes source field stability. Nothing first-party detects a breaking schema change before it propagates.

06.8 — per-engine run history is good, but the work-queue console, dead-letter resubmission and source-to-destination reconciliation are custom. Partly a consequence of choosing a SQL-backed queue — Service Bus provides dead-letter mechanics natively.

Three design constraints, stated plainly

  • Delivery is at-least-once; destination writes must be idempotent against a defined key
  • Work items must bind to the mapping version used, so a retry cannot silently adopt a newer transform
  • Reconciliation must compare source intent to destination state, not to worker-reported success
"Where the worker is an agent it writes into a production ERP rather than advising, and needs least privilege, approval thresholds and a kill switch."

Also surfaced: a data sensitivity label does not travel with a record once it is written into a destination ERP. The governance plane stops at the boundary.

These are the sentences that earn the artifact its keep. None of them were in the source brief — they fell out of forcing a real design through a structured model. The agent quote generalises well beyond this one initiative: as automation moves from advising to acting, "least privilege, approval thresholds, kill switch" becomes a standing design requirement — and it is the line most likely to start a conversation in an FSI room.
The outcome

It shipped — and it is maintainable

Published

A git repository publishing to GitHub Pages, deploying automatically on push.

One self-contained file. No CDN, no external scripts, no web fonts — so it renders identically from a local file, from OneDrive/SharePoint preview, and from the public site.

Any change that introduces an external reference breaks that property — which is why it's written down as a design constraint.

The method

How the work actually divided

What Scout did

  • Kept the model consistent while editing any part of it — caught overlaps across distant areas
  • Researched product mappings, components and licensing against Learn
  • Wrote candid positioning for every entry, with named competitors
  • Built and debugged the SPA — including a selector collision causing a false "No matches"
  • Read a MIP-protected brief under my identity and mapped it to the model
  • Re-audited for stale product facts across the whole set, twice

What stayed mine

  • Recognising that four finished artifacts were the wrong shape
  • The reframe from function to capability
  • The inclusion test — and rejecting the weaker one
  • The Practice distinction
  • Spotting that a magnet category wanted a lens, not a folder
  • Noticing that 141 correct rows were unreadable at a glance
  • Deciding what to leave out
  • Judging when honest was more valuable than favourable

The agent did the work that scales. I did the work that decides. Neither list would have produced this alone.

In effort terms this is about a dozen evenings across nine days. Built the traditional way — 141 capabilities researched, mapped, positioned, sourced and published — it is a multi-week engagement.

The agent didn't make the model better than that. It made iteration cheap enough to throw four versions away on day one — and that is where the quality actually came from.

If you keep one slide from the deck, keep this one. It's an honest division of labour that neither over-claims for the tool nor pretends the human was just a typist. Say the effort line plainly and then stop — don't dress it up. The claim is affordable iteration, not speed for its own sake, and that framing survives scrutiny where "10x faster" would not.
Transferable lessons

What I'd tell someone starting tomorrow

1Build the wrong thing quickly to find out what the right thing is. Four discarded artifacts in one evening taught me more than a week of planning would have.
2Fix the unit of the model before the content. Function → capability changed everything downstream and cost nothing to change early.
3Complete is not the same as comprehensible. An agent optimises for coverage — noticing the result is unreadable is a human job.
4Generate views from the data. If a picture can drift from the model, it eventually will.
5Scope discipline is the human’s job. An agent will always add. Treasury, tax and travel & expense are out of the model on purpose — and saying so is what keeps it a standard rather than a catalogue.
All five are tool-agnostic — the audience should leave with a method, not a product recommendation. These are the ones that survive being said in a single sentence. The two sharper modelling rules deliberately are not repeated here: capture the rule, not just the result and facet, not folder already have full slides earlier, and compressing them to one line strips the setup that made them land. If someone asks for the modelling lessons, go back to slides 6 and 7 rather than trying to restate them.
Where it goes next

What this unlocks — and what's still open

Unlocked

  • A new business can be assessed against the same standard, so results compare across the portfolio
  • Tiers let a small business be assessed against Essential rather than all 141
  • Coverage ratings make "what do we already own" and "what must we buy" one view
  • Any future initiative can be mapped the way the Fabric one was

Still open

  • Only one reference implementation so far — the pattern wants two or three
  • No assessment output format yet: the model describes the standard, not a scored estate
  • Product facts need periodic re-audit; the footer dates the model to August 2026
  • Capability tiering is a judgement that deserves a second reviewer
Ending with real open threads signals the standard is in service, not on a shelf. It also invites the room to contribute rather than just evaluate.
In closing

The goal was never a document.
It was a reusable part.

Four discarded starts · one reframe · 141 capabilities · 15 areas · 26 honest gaps · 24 commits · one published, self-contained standard — with a real initiative mapped against it to prove it works.

The measure of success isn't this artifact. It's that the next business joining the portfolio gets assessed in days rather than weeks — against the same standard, with comparable results.

Close on the reusability claim, then offer to open the live site. Letting someone pick an area and drill in beats any summary you could give.
